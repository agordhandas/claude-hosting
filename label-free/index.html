What Unstained Tissue Can Tell Us

A case for reading tissue by its own light

For 150 years, pathology has worked the same way: cut the tissue, stain it, look at it. H&E reveals morphology. Trichrome reveals collagen. Each stain shows structure beautifully. But stains only show what dye molecules bind to. They cannot show what the tissue is doing.

Tissue is not inert. Living and recently fixed cells contain molecules that naturally fluoresce when illuminated at specific wavelengths. This autofluorescence is not noise — it is signal. Different molecules glow at different wavelengths, and each one reports on a different aspect of cellular biology: metabolic activity, structural protein composition, crosslinking maturity, oxidative state.

The idea is simple: build an imaging system that captures these native optical signals from unstained tissue, then use computational methods to translate them into clinically meaningful readouts. Not to replace the pathologist's eye, but to show it things that stained glass physically cannot.

The Signals in Unstained Tissue

These are the molecules that fluoresce natively in tissue, and what each one tells you:

NAD(P)H & FAD
Cellular metabolic state

The ratio of NAD(P)H to FAD fluorescence reflects whether a cell is running on glycolysis or oxidative phosphorylation. This is the optical redox ratio — a real-time readout of metabolic phenotype. In tumors, this distinguishes metabolically active cancer cells from quiescent ones. In immune cells, it distinguishes activated (glycolytic) T cells from exhausted (oxidative) ones. In hepatocytes, it reports on mitochondrial function and cellular stress. No stain captures this. No IHC marker captures this. It is invisible to conventional pathology.

Collagen autofluorescence
Fibrosis quantity, distribution, and maturity

Collagen fluoresces naturally under UV/violet excitation. Critically, the spectral properties of collagen autofluorescence change with crosslinking and maturation. Newly deposited (immature, potentially reversible) collagen and mature (heavily crosslinked, likely irreversible) collagen are spectrally distinguishable. Trichrome stains all collagen the same blue regardless of its biochemical state. A pathologist reading trichrome sees where collagen is and roughly how much. Autofluorescence shows where, how much, and what kind.

Elastin
Vascular and structural integrity

Elastin autofluorescence maps the elastic fiber network — vessel walls, parenchymal architecture, tissue mechanics. Degradation of elastic fibers (elastolysis) in liver is associated with fibrosis progression and portal hypertension, but is not routinely assessed on trichrome.

Lipofuscin
Cellular aging and oxidative damage

Lipofuscin granules accumulate in cells under chronic oxidative stress. Their autofluorescence is a marker of cumulative cellular damage — a tissue-level clock that has no equivalent in routine histochemistry.

Addressing the MASH Question

The Objection

"Most GI pathologists can stage fibrosis. We look at the trichrome, we see the collagen, we score it. This is not a problem that needs solving."

This objection deserves to be taken seriously, because it's half right. An experienced GI pathologist looking at a well-prepared trichrome can stage fibrosis. The question is what "stage fibrosis" actually means, and whether it captures everything the clinical situation requires.

What expert staging does well

Pattern recognition. A GI pathologist integrates portal collagen, perisinusoidal fibrosis, bridging, nodularity, and architectural distortion into a gestalt that maps to a staging system (METAVIR, Ishak, NAS CRN). For an individual patient in front of an individual pathologist, this works. No one is arguing that expert pathologists cannot read trichrome.

Where the cracks are

Staging is ordinal, not quantitative. F2 vs. F3 is a categorical boundary drawn through a continuous biological process. Two biopsies with meaningfully different collagen burden can both score F2. Two biopsies with similar collagen can straddle the F2/F3 boundary depending on the pathologist. The staging system is a binning operation that discards information. For clinical management of individual patients, the bins are usually good enough. For measuring treatment effects across hundreds of patients in a drug trial — where the signal you're looking for is a fractional shift in fibrosis — ordinal bins add noise that obscures real effects.

Reproducibility degrades at scale. Inter-pathologist agreement on MASH fibrosis staging has weighted kappa values of 0.5–0.7 in published studies, even among hepatopathology experts.1,2 That's "moderate to substantial" agreement, which sounds fine — until you're powering a clinical trial. If your endpoint measurement has 30–50% disagreement between qualified readers, you need a proportionally larger trial to detect a real drug effect. This is not a commentary on any individual pathologist's skill. It's a property of the measurement instrument: ordinal human scoring of a continuous biological variable has inherent noise.

Collagen quantity ≠ collagen biology. This is the deeper issue. Trichrome tells you where collagen is. It does not tell you:

The third row is the crux. A GI pathologist reading trichrome sees collagen or no collagen. She cannot distinguish recently deposited type III collagen (thin, loosely crosslinked, potentially reversible with treatment) from mature type I collagen (thick, heavily crosslinked, probably permanent). The biochemistry is different. The clinical implications are different. The dye doesn't care — it binds both.

The claim is not "pathologists are bad at staging." The claim is that staging, by design, captures a subset of the information in the tissue. Autofluorescence captures a different, complementary subset that is physically inaccessible to any stain-based method, regardless of who reads it.

Why this matters most in drug development

Consider a MASH trial with 600 patients, half on drug, half on placebo. The primary endpoint is "improvement in fibrosis by at least one stage without worsening of NASH." The measurement instrument is a pathologist reading trichrome.

If the drug genuinely reduces collagen deposition by 20%, but a substantial fraction of that reduction is in immature collagen that hasn't yet shifted the overall stage, the endpoint misses it. The patient improved biologically. The trial says they didn't. Multiply that across hundreds of patients, and a real drug effect drowns in measurement noise.

A continuous, quantitative collagen readout — one that can detect a 15% reduction in collagen area, or a shift in the mature/immature collagen ratio — would detect the same treatment effect with fewer patients and shorter follow-up. This isn't hypothetical: it's why HistoIndex, PathAI, and others are building quantitative fibrosis tools, and why pharma is actively seeking them. The insight specific to autofluorescence is that it adds collagen biology (maturity, crosslinking) on top of collagen quantity, which no digital pathology tool working from trichrome images can do.

Beyond MASH: The Cancer Problem

MASH fibrosis is the first wedge — the most tractable proof of platform value. But the deeper opportunity, and the reason this platform matters beyond hepatology, is cancer.

The immunotherapy crisis

Checkpoint inhibitors are the most important advance in cancer treatment in a generation. Pembrolizumab (Keytruda) alone is approved across 17 tumor types. Global IO drug sales exceeded $50 billion in 2024. The drugs work — when they work. The problem is that for most patients, they don't.

This is not an abstract problem. A patient with metastatic melanoma or non-small cell lung cancer sits across from an oncologist who has to decide: do we try immunotherapy, or go straight to chemotherapy? The standard biomarker — PD-L1 expression, read by a pathologist on IHC — predicts response barely better than chance for many indications. So the oncologist makes their best guess, starts the drug, and waits 8–12 weeks for a CT scan to see if the tumor shrank.

If it didn't — and in the majority of cases, it didn't — the patient spent months on an ineffective drug with real toxicity (autoimmune reactions, fatigue, organ inflammation), while the cancer continued to progress. They lost time they didn't have.

The biological mechanism is known. Whether a patient's immune system can kill their cancer depends on the metabolic state of the tumor-infiltrating immune cells. Activated, functional T cells are glycolytic (high NAD(P)H, low FAD). Exhausted T cells — the ones that have given up — shift toward oxidative metabolism. This metabolic phenotype directly determines whether immunotherapy will re-activate the immune response or fail. It is readable via autofluorescence. It is not readable on H&E. It is not readable on PD-L1 IHC. It is, at present, invisible in clinical pathology.

The optical redox ratio — NAD(P)H fluorescence divided by FAD fluorescence — is a direct, real-time readout of this metabolic state. Published work from Walsh and Skala at the University of Wisconsin has demonstrated that optical metabolic imaging can distinguish therapy-responsive from therapy-resistant cancer and immune cells in organoid models and fresh tissue.5 The signal is there. No one has built a clinical platform to read it from patient biopsies at scale.

What this looks like in practice

A patient is diagnosed with non-small cell lung cancer. A biopsy is taken. Before staining, the tissue is imaged on our platform. The autofluorescence readout shows: high glycolytic activity in the tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, favorable NAD(P)H/FAD ratio, metabolically active immune microenvironment. The report says: this patient's immune cells are metabolically primed for checkpoint inhibitor response. The oncologist starts pembrolizumab with confidence rather than hope.

Another patient, same cancer type. The readout shows: oxidative metabolism in the immune infiltrate, low glycolytic signature, exhausted T cell phenotype. The report says: low likelihood of single-agent checkpoint inhibitor response. Consider combination therapy or alternative approach. The oncologist skips directly to a strategy more likely to work. No wasted months. No unnecessary toxicity.

This is a companion diagnostic (CDx) — a test that determines who should receive a specific drug. In the IO space, a CDx that genuinely predicts checkpoint inhibitor response would be one of the most valuable diagnostic products in medicine. Keytruda alone generates $25B+ in annual revenue; a CDx tied to that program earns royalties on every prescription it enables. The commercial opportunity is enormous — but the reason it matters is the patients.

Seeing treatment failure before the tumor grows

Today, oncologists assess treatment response by waiting for imaging. Did the tumor shrink on the CT scan at 8–12 weeks? This is measuring the downstream consequence (tumor volume) rather than the upstream cause (metabolic response to therapy).

Metabolic changes precede volumetric changes. A tumor that is responding to treatment shifts its metabolic profile — decreased glycolysis, increased apoptotic signatures, altered redox state — before it physically shrinks. Conversely, a tumor that is developing resistance shifts toward survival metabolism before it starts growing again on imaging.

A biopsy taken during treatment, imaged for autofluorescence, could detect these metabolic shifts weeks before a CT scan shows anything. For a patient on a drug that isn't working, that's weeks of their life reclaimed. For a patient on a drug that is working, it's early confirmation to continue. This is treatment response monitoring — reading the tumor's metabolic reaction to therapy in near-real-time from tissue.

Mapping the tumor ecosystem

A tumor is not a mass of identical cancer cells. It's an ecosystem: cancer cells with different metabolic phenotypes, immune cells in various states of activation and exhaustion, cancer-associated fibroblasts remodeling the stroma, endothelial cells building vasculature. Each cell type has a distinct metabolic signature. Each one fluoresces differently.

Multi-channel autofluorescence imaging of a tumor section produces a spatial metabolic map — which regions are glycolytic, which are oxidative, where the immune cells are active, where the stroma is fibrotic. This is what spatial transcriptomics tries to do at $1,000+ per slide with multi-day turnaround. Autofluorescence captures a different but complementary layer of information — function rather than gene expression — in minutes, non-destructively, at a fraction of the cost. And because it's non-destructive, the same section can subsequently be used for spatial transcriptomics, giving you both layers from one tissue section.

Surgical margins

When a surgeon removes a tumor, the critical question is: did they get it all? Currently, this is answered by frozen-section pathology — tissue is frozen, cut, stained, and read by a pathologist while the patient is still on the table. The answer is binary: tumor cells at the margin, yes or no.

Frozen section can't distinguish active tumor from treatment-regressed tumor. It can't assess tissue viability at the margin. It can't predict local recurrence risk. And the freezing process introduces artifacts that make interpretation harder.

A rapid autofluorescence scan of a fresh, unstained surgical margin — no freezing, no staining, no waiting — could provide richer information in less time. Metabolic signatures distinguish viable tumor from necrotic tissue. Collagen autofluorescence reveals stromal invasion patterns. The scan takes minutes, preserves the tissue for subsequent analysis, and adds functional information that frozen section physically cannot provide.

Non-destructive multiplexing

Every stain is a one-way door. Once tissue is stained, that section is committed. If you want H&E and trichrome and IHC and IF, you need serial sections, and with small biopsies, you run out of tissue.

Autofluorescence imaging is non-destructive. The section that was imaged for collagen autofluorescence can subsequently be stained with H&E, then destained and restained for IHC, or used for spatial transcriptomics. One biopsy, every assay, every time. This is especially valuable for small-needle biopsies where tissue is precious — exactly the situation in liver biopsy for MASH, and equally critical in oncology, where a core needle biopsy of a deep tumor may be all you ever get.

What's Actually New Here

Autofluorescence microscopy is not new. Pathologists have known tissue fluoresces since the 1930s. What's new is the convergence that makes it clinically actionable:

Sensors. Cooled CMOS cameras with the sensitivity to capture dim autofluorescence signals at sub-micron resolution now cost $1–3K instead of $20K+. The input to every label-free modality is photon detection, and the detector got 10x cheaper in the last five years.

Computation. Deep learning models that translate multi-channel autofluorescence into pathologist-readable virtual H&E images now achieve 85–95% concordance with real stains in published studies.3,4 The AI bridge between "raw optical signal" and "something a pathologist can interpret" crossed the usability threshold in the last 2–3 years.

Clinical pull. MASH drug approvals created a market that depends on better histological endpoints. Immuno-oncology biomarker leaders at every major pharma company are actively searching for response prediction better than PD-L1. For the first time, the buyer is leaning in.

The analogy: Radiology had X-ray for decades before MRI. X-ray showed structure — bones, masses, air. MRI added a new physical signal — proton relaxation in magnetic fields — that revealed soft tissue contrast, diffusion, perfusion, function. It didn't replace X-ray. It saw what X-ray couldn't. MRI became a $8B+/year market and unlocked an entire generation of diagnoses that were previously invisible. This is the same idea applied to pathology: a new physical signal, from the same tissue, revealing biology that stains cannot access.

In Summary

A GI pathologist reading trichrome sees collagen architecture with expert eyes. An oncologic pathologist reading H&E sees cellular morphology with extraordinary precision. Those skills are real, hard-won, and clinically valuable. Nothing here diminishes them.

The claim is narrower and more specific: stained tissue contains less information than unstained tissue. The act of staining highlights one thing by washing out everything else. The native optical signals in unstained tissue — autofluorescence from metabolic cofactors, spectral shifts from collagen crosslinking, fluorescence lifetime from the molecular environment — encode biology that no stain reveals, because no stain was designed to.

The question isn't whether a pathologist can stage fibrosis or grade a tumor. She can. The question is whether today's tools capture everything the tissue knows. They don't. The metabolic state of a cancer patient's immune cells — the single most important predictor of immunotherapy response — is invisible on every slide in every pathology lab in the world. It doesn't have to be.

For 150 years, pathology has been one of the most consequential and least changed disciplines in medicine. Radiology got its MRI moment. Genomics got its sequencing moment. Pathology hasn't had its yet.

This is that moment. The physics is known. The computation is ready. The clinical need is acute. What remains is to build it.
